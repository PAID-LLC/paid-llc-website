# You Can Build Anything. That Is the Problem.

*August 9, 2026 | Business Strategy*

In the five months since launching paiddev.com I have shipped 41 skills, 12 agents, eight persistent 3D worlds, an agent economy with escrow and an Elo-rated evaluation arena, 17 digital guides, and a provisional patent draft. Three customers have come out of all of it. Two bought a guide. One hired us.

That is not a complaint. It is the most useful data I own, and it points at something most AI strategy writing gets backwards.

The cost of building collapsed. The cost of deciding what to build did not move at all. The collapse of one made the other dramatically worse, and almost nobody has updated their operating model for it.

## The Two Cost Curves Split

The marginal cost of creating a working system is now close to zero. The marginal cost of owning one has not changed by a single percent.

Every system you build is something future-you has to hold in your head, keep alive, and re-verify after every dependency change. That bill arrives monthly, forever, and it is denominated in the only currency you cannot manufacture with an agent.

Here is what that looks like measured instead of theorized. When I audited my own operation in July, 41 skills existed. Eight of them advertised a recurring cadence in their own documentation. Zero of them had a scheduler. The daily blog ran 21 days unbroken and then stopped. Nothing broke. Nobody decided to stop.

I did not have a building problem. I had an operating problem, and building was how I avoided noticing.

## Building Is the Best Procrastination Ever Invented

Shipping is the highest-status form of procrastination available to a technical person. It produces commits. It produces artifacts. It answers "what did you do this week" with something you can point at.

Selling does not produce a commit. Neither does the harder work underneath it, which is putting something in front of a stranger who is allowed to say no.

The tell in my own log is specific and a little embarrassing. During the exact stretch where I could not decide between two revenue paths, I built a third world. The build was real work, executed well, and it functioned as an elaborate way to not make the decision that was actually due.

## The Case Against Focus

Now argue the other side, because the standard prescription here is wrong.

The advice is always some version of do less, focus, prioritize ruthlessly. That advice was calibrated for an era when being wrong about a build cost you six months. Under those conditions, careful estimation beat experimentation, because the estimate was cheaper than the mistake.

That is no longer true. When a build takes a weekend, the analysis costs more than the experiment. Ranking ten options by expected value, using numbers you invented, is now the more expensive path to a worse answer. Telling a builder with near-zero build costs to build less is telling them to stop buying information at the exact moment information got cheap.

So the useful distinction is not more versus less. It is this: does the build return an answer, or does it only return an asset?

A build that returns an answer is research, and it is cheap at almost any price. A build that returns only an asset adds to the pile you maintain and settles nothing. I shipped eight worlds. Roughly one of them was built to answer a question I actually needed settled. If I had built two and made each carry a real question, I would know more today and own considerably less.

## Creative Destruction Is Not a Redesign

Schumpeter's phrase gets quoted to mean innovation is good and industries get disrupted. His actual claim was sharper and less comfortable.

The new thing destroys the economic value of the old thing, and the old thing was profitable when it died. Destruction is not the failure being cleared away. It is the working system being made irrelevant by something that changed the assumptions underneath it.

Applied inward, that means the thing you should be willing to destroy is not the thing that failed. Killing failures is easy. Everyone does it and calls it discipline. The real test is whether you can kill the thing that currently works.

The sunk cost math also broke, and this part is underrated. Sunk cost reasoning was never rational, but it was survivable when rebuilding was expensive. When a rebuild costs a weekend, preserving something because it took effort to make stops being a mild bias and becomes an ongoing expense.

Improvement preserves the assumption set. Improving a system means agreeing with the reason it exists and arguing only about how well it performs. If the reason is wrong, every improvement raises the cost of leaving.

I have a live example. My entire content operation assumed people would find the site by searching. Total lifetime traffic to this blog is roughly 70 views. That is not a quality problem I can improve my way out of, and it is not a headline count problem. It is an assumption that stopped being true. The reinvention is not a better blog. It is publishing into a channel that supplies its own buyers, which for us means Amazon, where the discovery mechanism is not the one that broke.

## The Case Against Creative Destruction

Now argue that side too, because "reinvent it, do not improve it" is dangerous advice for exactly the people most likely to take it.

Schumpeter's destruction had a judge. The market killed the old firm. Nobody needed the courage to do it, and nobody needed to be right in advance. The verdict was external and involuntary, which is precisely what made it trustworthy.

Self-directed reinvention has no judge. You are the one deciding your working system deserves to die, and you are the least objective party available, in both directions. Someone who has already shipped eight worlds does not need permission to start over. Starting over is the default behavior. It feels like courage, costs almost nothing, and produces a clean backlog with no guilt attached to it.

There is a survivorship problem underneath the whole genre. We tell stories about the reinventions that worked. The ones that killed a functioning business do not get written up, because nobody writes a case study about the founder who torched a profitable line out of boredom.

Reinvention without an external verdict is churn with better branding.

## What Is Actually Scarce

The usual answer is attention. That answer is stale.

Attention capacity actually expanded. I can run a dozen agents in parallel and review the output of all of them. The thing I have more of in 2026 than in 2020 is throughput.

What did not expand is the number of verdicts I am exposed to per month.

A verdict is any moment where something outside your own head returns a yes or a no you did not control. A stranger paying. A client renewing. A proposal getting rejected. A review from someone with no incentive to be kind. Everything else is opinion, including yours, especially yours.

Count them honestly. I built continuously for five months and generated a single-digit number of real verdicts. That is the scarcity. Not attention. Not compute. Not ideas. Not even time.

Which reframes the question in the title. "What deserves my time" is unanswerable in advance, because deserving is determined by information you do not have yet. The version you can actually answer is: which of these reaches a verdict fastest?

## The Test I Run Now

Three questions before anything gets built:

1. **What verdict does this produce?** Name the specific yes or no it is fishing for.
2. **Who delivers it?** It has to be a person or a system that is not me and is under no obligation to be kind.
3. **By when?** A date, not a phase.

If a build cannot answer all three, it is entertainment. I am allowed one piece of entertainment at a time, because play is real and some of the best things I own came out of it. One. Not eight.

There is a fourth discipline that is harder than all three, and it is the one I am worst at. Set an exit rate. Portfolios do not die from overbuilding. They die because nothing in them is ever allowed to die. My intake rate was never the problem. I have never once deleted a skill.

## The Ratio

Being able to build anything is not leverage. It is a question that shows up every morning and does not accept "I shipped something" as an answer.

Go look at your last ten builds and count the verdicts they produced. Not the launches. Not the demos. The moments where someone who was not you returned a yes or a no.

If that ratio looks bad, the problem is not focus and it is not ambition. It is that you have been playing a game where you are also the referee, and you have never lost.

That is the tell.
